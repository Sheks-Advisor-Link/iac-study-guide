2 · What I learnt about where the line sits (C vs BC vs LC)

Candidate 2 = Competent — why

  • Answered every part of every task, and applied to Cold Press specifically (not generic theory).
  • Communication & format were excellent — proper report for Task 5, clear headings, right tone. This genuinely moves grades.
  • Defensible judgement with a clear recommendation each time; minor technical gaps (missed s10(1)(h)/s8F, the labelling sign) were forgiven because the whole was competent.

Candidate 1 = Below Competent — why

  • Time management — Task 5 not attempted (NA) and Task 6 only half done. You can't be competent on tasks you don't finish.
  • Irrelevant dumping — a large s24O discussion in Task 2 (the "net assets not shares" trap), and a too-"IFRS-y" Task 3 when the bank wanted enterprise value.
  • Incomplete — no inventory note (Task 7); no path forward in the ethics task (Task 4).

Candidate 3 = Below Competent (borderline) — why

  • Good communication throughout — every response was easy to follow and well-pitched (this earned the three C's).
  • But fundamental technical/calc errors capped it: the Task 1 modification test was wrong, and Task 5 didn't compute the requested margins. Good writing can't rescue a wrong number.
  • Incomplete technical coverage — Task 6 omitted VAT (a vendor!) and employees' tax.

3 · The one place I differed from UCT (and what it taught me)

Candidate 2, Task 5 — I said C, UCT said HC. The candidate's presentation, report format and strategic/risk factors were outstanding, but their Option 2 cumulative calculation looked materially off (operating margin ~11% vs the solution's ~1%) and it drove a different recommendation (Option 2 instead of Option 1). I wanted to hold it at C on the calculation. UCT awarded HC — rewarding the strong, followable process and presentation and applying own-figure leniency to the calc. Lesson: examiners weight a clear, defensible, well-presented process very heavily — a sound method with a slip is treated far more kindly than a right answer with no workings. I was marking the number; UCT was marking the competence.

4 · What this taught me for my OWN exam technique

Marking others was the most useful part — the failures I penalised are exactly my own risk areas:

  • Finish everything (time management). Candidate 1 failed mainly by not attempting a task. An unattempted task is an automatic NA — I must budget time and get something down for every required.
  • Answer every part, and apply to the scenario. The single biggest divider between C and BC/LC. Re-read the required and tick off each sub-part.
  • Don't dump — stay in scope. Candidate 1's s24O and "IFRS-y" Task 3 cost real marks. Identify the trigger, then answer the question asked.
  • Show clear, followable workings and use the right format. Candidate 2 got HC on a calc with an error because the process was clear; Candidate 3 got BC partly because the margins weren't shown. Presentation is not decoration — it's competence.
  • Cover the technical breadth — VAT and employees' tax when tax is asked; the "small" items (labelling sign, s10(1)(h)); and always give a way forward on an ethics question.
Overall reflection. I can now recognise professional competence reliably — my grades matched UCT on 23/24 tasks and on all three overall outcomes, and the one difference taught me the most (process & presentation over the exact number). Seeing three real scripts side by side made the C/BC/LC boundary concrete: Competent = complete, applied, well-communicated, defensible — even if imperfect; Below Competent = unfinished, out of scope, or undermined by fundamental errors. Most importantly, marking others showed me my own development plan in someone else's handwriting: finish every task, stay in scope, and show the workings.

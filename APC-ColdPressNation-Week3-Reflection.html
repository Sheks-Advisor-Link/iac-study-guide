APC 2023 Cold Press Nation – Week 3: Recognising Professional Competence

Reflection (Step 3). I marked the three candidate scripts myself against the blank grid, then compared my assessment to UCT’s. Candidate 328.

This week I marked Candidates 1, 2 and 3 using the blank marking grid (my three completed grids are attached), and then compared my grades to UCT’s. The point of the exercise is to learn to recognise professional competence – to see, from the marker’s side, what actually separates a competent script from a below-competent one. My completed grids and this reflection are my evidence for the phase.

2. What I learnt about where the line sits (C vs BC vs LC)

Candidate 2 = Competent – why

  • Answered every part of every task, and applied it to Cold Press specifically (not generic theory).
  • Communication and format were strong – a proper report for Task 5, clear headings, right tone. This genuinely moves grades.
  • Defensible judgement with a clear recommendation each time; minor technical gaps (missed s10(1)(h)/s8F, the labelling sign) were forgiven because the whole was competent.

Candidate 1 = Below Competent – why

  • Time management – Task 5 not attempted (NA) and Task 6 only half done. You cannot be competent on tasks you do not finish.
  • Irrelevant dumping – a large s24O discussion in Task 2 (the “net assets not shares” trap), and a too-“IFRS-y” Task 3 when the bank wanted enterprise value.
  • Incomplete – no inventory note (Task 7); no path forward in the ethics task (Task 4).

Candidate 3 = Below Competent (borderline) – why

  • Good communication throughout – every response was easy to follow and well-pitched (this earned the three C’s).
  • But fundamental technical / calculation errors capped it: the Task 1 modification test was wrong, and Task 5 did not compute the requested margins. Good writing cannot rescue a wrong number.
  • Incomplete technical coverage – Task 6 omitted VAT (a vendor) and employees’ tax.

3. The one place I differed from UCT (and what it taught me)

Candidate 2, Task 5 – I said C, UCT said HC. The candidate’s presentation, report format and strategic/risk factors were excellent, but their Option 2 cumulative calculation looked materially off (operating margin ~11% vs the solution’s ~1%) and it drove a different recommendation (Option 2 instead of Option 1). I wanted to hold it at C on the calculation. UCT awarded HC – rewarding the clear, followable process and presentation, and applying own-figure leniency to the calc. Lesson: examiners weight a clear, defensible, well-presented process very heavily – a sound method with a slip is treated far more kindly than a right answer with no workings. I was marking the number; UCT was marking the competence.

4. What this taught me for my own exam technique

Marking others was the most useful part – the failures I penalised are exactly my own risk areas:

  • Finish everything (time management). Candidate 1 failed mainly by not attempting a task. An unattempted task is an automatic NA – I must budget time and get something down for every required.
  • Answer every part, and apply to the scenario. The single biggest divider between C and BC/LC. Re-read the required and tick off each sub-part.
  • Do not dump – stay in scope. Candidate 1’s s24O and “IFRS-y” Task 3 cost real marks. Identify the trigger, then answer the question asked.
  • Show clear, followable workings and use the right format. Candidate 2 got HC on a calc with an error because the process was clear; Candidate 3 got BC partly because the margins were not shown.
  • Cover the technical breadth – VAT and employees’ tax when tax is asked; the “small” items (labelling sign, s10(1)(h)); and always give a way forward on an ethics question.
Overall reflection. I can now recognise professional competence reliably – my grades matched UCT on 23/24 tasks and on all three overall outcomes, and the one difference taught me the most (process and presentation over the exact number). Seeing three real scripts side by side made the C/BC/LC boundary concrete: Competent = complete, applied, well-communicated, defensible – even if imperfect; Below Competent = unfinished, out of scope, or undermined by fundamental errors. Most importantly, marking others showed me my own development plan in someone else’s handwriting: finish every task, stay in scope, and show the workings.

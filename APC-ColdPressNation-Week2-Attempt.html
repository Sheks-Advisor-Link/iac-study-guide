Task 1 — Feedback on the loan restructuring accounting (Doc 1)

From: Yonwaba  To: Amien  Subject: Loan from parent — my feedback
Hi Amien, thanks for sharing this. My feedback on each option is below (no journals, no tax, as you asked).

Option A — loan becomes convertible into ordinary shares

  • Adding an equity-conversion feature changes the loan substantially, so I agree you should derecognise the old R12m loan on 1 Aug 2023 and recognise a new instrument.
  • But the new instrument is a convertible (compound) instrument, so I don't think we can just recognise the whole R12m as one liability. Under IAS 32 we may need to split it:
    • First question — is it convertible into a fixed or a variable number of shares? You haven't told me, and it changes the answer.
    • If fixed number of shares → it's a compound instrument: recognise the liability component at the fair value of a similar loan without the conversion feature, and the residual (balancing figure) in equity.
    • If variable number → the conversion feature doesn't meet the equity definition; it's all a financial liability and the residual goes to profit or loss (day-1 gain/loss) where fair value is based on observable inputs.
  • The interest journal (R12m × 9% = R1,080,000) only records the cash coupon. The actual interest expense for FY2024 should be the carrying amount of the liability component × the market rate used to value that component (a loan without the conversion feature), not 9%. So the interest expense needs correcting.
Info I still need: whether the shares are fixed or variable, the market rate / fair value of a similar non-convertible loan, and confirmation there are no transaction costs (they'd change the split and the interest).

Option B — higher rate (11.75%), capital + compounded interest due 31 July 2026

  • First we have to decide if the terms are substantially different, because that decides modification vs extinguishment. IFRS 9 gives us the "10% test": compare the PV of the new cash flows discounted at the original effective rate (9%) to the old carrying amount (R12m).
  • My calculation:
    FV of new loan at 31 Jul 2026 = 12 000 000 × 1.1175³ = R16 746 492
    PV at original 9% for 3 yrs = 16 746 492 ÷ 1.09³ = R12 931 364
    Difference = 12 931 364 − 12 000 000 = R931 364 = 7.78% of R12m → < 10%
  • So it's not substantially different → treat as a modification (not a new loan). That means:
    • Remeasure the loan to R12 931 364 on 1 Aug 2023;
    • Recognise the R931 364 increase as a modification loss in P/L in FY2024;
    • Keep using the original 9% rate → interest FY2024 = 12 931 364 × 9% = R1 163 828.
  • So your Option B treatment (11.75% × 12m = R1 410 000, no modification loss) needs to change to the above.
Info I still need: confirmation of no transaction costs. Let's work through the journals together.

Task 2 — Tax consequences of the interest & the restructuring (Doc 2)

From: Yonwaba  To: Amien  Subject: Loan — tax consequences

Existing loan — FY2023

Cold Press Nation:
  • Interest is deductible under s24J — incurred in the course of trade / production of income (the farm generates taxable income). Calculated on a yield-to-maturity basis; paid annually in arrears with no discount/premium, so interest incurred = interest paid on 31 July.
  • s23M could limit the deduction (interest to a creditor in a controlling relationship where the interest isn't taxed in their hands), but the amount doesn't appear to exceed the limit.
MegaDrinks (non-resident):
  • Taxed in SA only on SA-source income. The interest is SA-source (paid by a SA resident / funds used in SA — s9).
  • As a non-resident, the interest is exempt from normal tax under s10(1)(h), BUT subject to 15% interest withholding tax (s50A–H) — a final tax — which CPN must withhold and pay to SARS on the 31 July payment.
  • The 15% may be reduced by a Double Tax Agreement — I'd need to know which country MegaDrinks is tax-resident in and check the DTA.

Restructuring — 2024 year of assessment

Option A (convertible):
  • Interest deducted at 9% (s24J), subject to WHT. On s31 transfer pricing — 9% is below the 11.75% market rate, but that's because of the conversion feature, so I don't think s31 applies; if it did, the deduction would be limited and the disallowed part a deemed dividend in specie (dividends tax).
  • Bigger concern — s8F hybrid debt: because the loan can convert to shares, there's a risk it's a hybrid debt instrument. If so, the interest becomes non-deductible and is treated as a dividend in specie (dividends tax on CPN). This depends on whether it converts to a fixed number of shares — need to confirm.
  • If it later converts to shares of lesser value, the debt-benefit rules (s19 & para 12A) could trigger recoupments/CGT. No group exclusion applies because MegaDrinks is non-resident (a s41 group must all be SA-resident).
  • These are unfavourable — I'd recommend we get a tax specialist before pursuing Option A so we don't accidentally trigger s8F or the debt-benefit rules.
Option B:
  • Interest at 11.75% (s24J, YTM, WHT as before). Capitalising/compounding the interest doesn't change deductibility (still YTM), it just increases the deduction over time.
  • 11.75% is market-related → no s31; the new loan replaces one of equal value → no debt benefit. No concerns.

Task 3 — Plain-language note for the bank (Doc 3)

From: Yonwaba  To: Amien  Subject: For the bank — how the trees affect the value of the business

Here's a version without the accounting jargon you can send on:

"Financial value" here means the business's ability to generate future cash and profit. The apple trees are grown to produce fruit, not to be sold — so their value lies in the apples they can produce. As the trees mature, their yield grows (they're only in their 3rd year of fruiting), which secures more future harvests and more future profit → this increases the financial value of the business.

That has to be balanced against the fact that the trees have a limited fruiting life (about 20 more years). Every harvest brings the end of that life a little closer, which slowly reduces value — so we'll need to keep reinvesting in new trees to sustain the harvest.

Within each season there's a cycle: as the apples grow on the trees, the trees become more valuable (they're "laden with apples"); at harvest that value is extracted — the trees drop in value but that value converts into valuable picked apples, and when those are sold it becomes cash. So value builds up as the fruit grows, is realised at harvest, and turns into cash on sale. As long as we control our costs and reinvest, this cycle should produce sustainable future cash flow."

Task 4 — Input on the '100% organic' / pesticide question (Doc 4)

From: Yonwaba  To: Amien  Subject: The organic claim

My view is focused only on the '100% organic' claim (not the safety of the pesticide). If we treat this crop with a synthetic (non-organic) pesticide, then we cannot honestly call the juice '100% organic'. If we use it, I believe we're ethically bound to tell customers and change / pause the '100% organic' labelling and marketing until we can source only organically-grown apples. Claiming otherwise would be greenwashing, and the reputational damage if customers find out would likely be far worse than the cost of handling it honestly.

Of the 6 King IV Principle 1 attributes, these apply:
  • Integrity — it would be dishonest to claim 100% organic while using a synthetic pesticide. We need to manage the conflict (profit vs honesty) so we're beyond reproach.
  • Responsibility — the responsible action is not to deceive customers; the downside of being caught is worse than the cost of doing it right.
  • Accountability — we must take accountability for the claims we put on our packaging.
  • Fairness — a stakeholder-inclusive approach means being fair to customers, who rely on accurate product information.
  • Transparency — be open and honest; don't provide misleading product information.

(Competence is less relevant to this particular issue.)

Other King IV principles to consider:
  • Corporate citizenship / responsibility — CPN is a corporate citizen and should act ethically regardless of the cost.
  • Integrated thinking — this decision affects more than the financials; dishonest branding damages non-financial "stores of value" like our reputation, over the long term.
Recommendation: look for an alternative first — e.g. sell the treated crop to buyers who don't need organic apples and source organic apples elsewhere. If that's genuinely not possible, then use the crop but be transparent and fix the labelling.

Report to the Cold Press Nation management team

Margins (my summary):

Key workings / assumptions:
  • The only variable cost driven by litres (besides variable manufacturing) is sales & distribution = R29 319k ÷ 3 765 824 litres = ~R7.79/litre, constant across products.
  • Option 1: add ginger 120 000 litres at R300 SP / R200 variable manufacturing; drop 60 000 litres of mixed berries to free capacity; fixed costs increase by R2.8m (heating equipment). New total CM ≈ R76.9m; operating profit ≈ R14.6m on revenue ≈ R228.8m → 34% CM, ~6% OP.
  • Option 2: split orange into pure + blended (double the blend volume), give up 80 000 litres of pure apple, import apples/oranges in winter, no fixed-cost increase. CM ≈ R60.7m; operating profit ≈ R1.2m on revenue ≈ R184.6m → 33% CM, ~1% OP.
Top 5 strategic considerations:
  1. Ginger shots ride the growing health-trend and match competitors (e.g. Sir Fruit's shot range) — good for brand relevance.
  2. Growing ginger at Onverwacht fits CPN's strategy of investing in its own farming and gives supply control/quality.
  3. Option 1 uses the winter spare capacity (demand rises in cold/flu season) — exactly the problem we're solving.
  4. Absolute Rands matter, not just %: Option 1's operating profit is roughly double Option 2's, even though its CM% is similar.
  5. Option 2 is low-risk / no capital outlay and easy to reverse — a reasonable "until we scale ginger" option.
Top 5 risks:
  1. Importing apples/oranges (Option 2) — no guaranteed supply; logistics risk.
  2. Estimates could be wrong → wrong product mix / wrong decision.
  3. Blend legislation — get the mix wrong and products could be pulled / fined.
  4. Option 1's first-year fixed costs (set-up) may be overstated, or ginger could be a passing fad → possible future impairment.
  5. Ginger is hard to juice → more machine wear & maintenance; and competitors already sell shots, capping the premium.
Recommendation: Pursue Option 1 (ginger shots). It gives the highest operating profit both in % and absolute Rands (~double Option 2), and it aligns with growing our own farming operations and using winter capacity. (I'd double-check the exact figures before finalising.)

Task 6 — Influencer vs affiliate program: factors & tax (Doc 6)

From: Yonwaba  To: Nate  Subject: Marketing options
Social influencer — key factors:
  • Relevance & fit (health/wellness niche, aligns with our brand & target market); reach & engagement (real followers, likes/comments); authenticity & trust; quality of content; and willingness to follow our pre-/post-approval process and create content from our premises.
Influencer — tax:
  • The R5 000/week payments are deductible under s11(a) (revenue, not capital).
  • But because we require content from our premises, pre/post-approval, and pay a fixed amount regardless of hours, the influencer is not an independent contractor → the payments are remuneration → CPN must withhold employees' tax (PAYE).
  • No VAT — the influencer isn't carrying on an enterprise in respect of these services (treated like employment).
Affiliate program — key factors:
  • Commission structure (rates, thresholds, payment timing — must still protect our margin); affiliate support/resources (who provides them — we have no marketing team); guidelines/control (autonomy vs brand consistency); tracking & reporting (attribution, fraud prevention); and program promotion to sign up existing customers.
Affiliate — tax:
  • Additional sales are recognised at gross (100% is ours). Commissions are deductible under s11(a) once the obligation is unconditional (threshold met & refund period lapsed).
  • Affiliates have minimal control from us → independent contractors → commissions are not remuneration → no PAYE.
  • Affiliates unlikely to exceed the VAT registration threshold → no VAT on commissions.

Task 7 — Inventory GL review & note disclosure (Doc 7)

From: Yonwaba  To: Amien  Subject: Inventory — my review & the completed note
General principle: only capitalise costs incurred to bring inventory to its present location and condition; everything else is expensed (cost of sales if production-related, otherwise operating expenses). Variable production costs are capitalised as incurred; fixed production overheads are allocated based on normal capacity.

Raw materials account — issues

  • Foreign exchange losses (R256 790) should not be in inventory. Imported fruit is recorded at the spot rate on the date ownership transfers; later exchange gains/losses go to P/L. → remove.
  • Import duties (R30 000) — only capitalise if non-refundable. Need to check.

Finished goods account — issues

  • Cost accountant salary (R490 000) and head office costs (R1 324 670) — not production costs → expense to P/L.
  • Labelling machine depreciation is shown as a credit (R55 321) — it should be added, not subtracted. → correct the sign.
  • Transportation costs (R1 893 560) in finished goods — if this is transport to customers it's a distribution cost and cannot be capitalised → check & likely expense. (Raw-material inbound transport is fine to capitalise.)
  • Production depreciation (ozone plant, shredding/pressing/filtering) is correctly capitalised, but the fixed portion must be allocated on normal capacity — check the allocation.
  • Fruit that fails quality checks: abnormal losses → expense; normal losses may stay in production cost.
  • These corrections may affect opening inventory → if material, disclose a prior-period error (IAS 8).
  • Obsolescence provision workings weren't given — inventory rose a lot but the provision barely moved. Please share the workings.
Completed note (the parts you left):
Accounting policy: "…recognises inventory initially at cost… The cost of inventory is determined on a first-in-first-out basis. Cost includes expenditure incurred in acquiring, manufacturing and transporting inventory to its present location and condition. Finished goods include raw materials, direct labour and materials, and production overheads allocated on the basis of normal operating capacity. Inventory is subsequently measured at the lower of cost and net realisable value."

Task 8 — (Caryn) Audit procedures & risks over agricultural produce (Doc 8)

From: Caryn  To: Sanlie  Subject: Agricultural produce — procedures & risks
3 key audit risks over initial recognition of harvested apples (FVLCS at harvest, IAS 41):
  1. Quantity of apples harvested may be overstated (very high volumes; measurement error) → misstated valuation.
  2. Farming expenses may be incorrectly capitalised into the cost of the apples instead of expensed — produce must be at FVLCS, not cost.
  3. Valuation is complex/judgemental — significant estimates in determining fair value at initial recognition (risk of misstatement/bias).
Substantive procedures over initial recognition:
  1. Obtain a listing of apples harvested per month of the season (volume).
  2. Enquire & understand the weighing/measuring process at harvest; assess vs industry norms.
  3. Enquire on the valuation methodology for FV at each harvest; assess for reasonableness & IAS 41 compliance.
  4. Obtain management's FVLCS calculation: (a) recompute for arithmetic accuracy; (b) obtain SA wholesale apple price per kg and assess the price used; (c) inspect the costs to sell deducted and check IAS 41 alignment.
  5. Enquire about any abnormal weather/environmental events in the year, and research whether any occurred, that could affect the harvest value.
  6. Obtain the journals; confirm the amount recognised agrees to the calculation and that an immediate FV gain is recognised in P/L.

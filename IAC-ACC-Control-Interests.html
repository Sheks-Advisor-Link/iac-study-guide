Control Interests — Subsidiaries, Associates & Joint Arrangements

How to classify an investment & answer discussion questions · IFRS 10 · IAS 28 · IFRS 11 · SAICA IAC 2026 · T. Van Heerden
The one question every scenario is really asking: "How much power does the investor have over the investee?" The answer puts the investment in one of four boxes, and each box has its own accounting. Your job in a discussion question is to test each requirement of the relevant standard against the facts, conclude, and state the treatment.

2 · IFRS 10 — Control (Subsidiary)

Definition: an investor controls an investee when it is exposed to, or has rights to, variable returns from its involvement AND has the ability to affect those returns through its power over the investee. All THREE elements must be present.

Requirement 1 — POWER over the investee

Existing rights that give the current ability to direct the "relevant activities" (the activities that significantly affect the investee's returns — e.g. sales, purchasing, funding, appointing key management).

Say POWER is met when the scenario shows…
  • >50% of the voting rights (and votes decide the relevant activities); or
  • De facto control — a large holding (e.g. 40–49%) while the rest is widely dispersed and the investor has consistently elected the majority of the board;
  • Substantive potential voting rights — options/convertibles that are currently exercisable and not deeply out-of-the-money;
  • Contractual rights — a shareholders' agreement / right to appoint, remove or direct the board or key management;
  • rights are substantive (can actually be exercised), not merely protective (e.g. veto on liquidation or above-budget capex = protective → does NOT give power).

Requirement 2 — EXPOSURE / RIGHTS TO VARIABLE RETURNS

The investor's returns must have the potential to vary with the investee's performance.

Say VARIABLE RETURNS is met when… the investor receives dividends, changes in the investment's value, management/service fees, synergies, cost savings, or returns not available to other holders. (Returns can be positive or negative.)

Requirement 3 — LINK: ability to use power to affect returns

The investor must be a principal (acting for itself), not an agent (acting for others).

Say the LINK is met (principal) when… the investor can appoint/remove the decision-maker, sets strategy, and its returns are significant & variable. Agent (no control) when a manager holds a small stake, earns a market-related fee, and can be removed without cause by others.
Conclusion & treatmentAll three present → subsidiary → consolidate from the date control is obtained; recognise goodwill & NCI (IFRS 3); de-consolidate when control is lost.

3 · IAS 28 — Significant Influence (Associate)

Definition: the power to participate in the financial and operating policy decisions of the investee, but NOT control or joint control over those policies.

The presumptions (your starting point)

  • ≥20% of voting rights → presumed to have significant influence (rebut only with clear evidence it doesn't).
  • <20% → presumed NOT to have significant influence (rebut with evidence that it does).
Say SIGNIFICANT INFLUENCE is met when the scenario shows any of…
  • 20%–50% of the voting power (the presumption); OR <20% but with the indicators below;
  • Representation on the board of directors;
  • Participation in policy-making processes — incl. decisions about dividends/distributions;
  • Material transactions between the investor and investee;
  • Interchange of managerial personnel; or
  • provision of essential technical information.
Conclusion & treatment"Participate but not control" → associate → equity method: carry at cost + share of post-acquisition profit/OCI − dividends received; test for impairment.

4 · IFRS 11 — Joint Control & Joint Arrangements

A joint arrangement is an arrangement of which two or more parties have joint control. It is either a joint operation or a joint venture — the difference is entirely about the parties' rights and obligations, not the label they give it.

TypeThe parties have…Accounting
Joint operation (JO)rights to the ASSETS and obligations for the LIABILITIES (joint operators)Each recognises its own share of assets, liabilities, revenue & expenses.
Joint venture (JV)rights to the NET ASSETS only (joint venturers)Equity method (IAS 28).

Step 1 — Is there joint control? (gateway — both must hold)

Say JOINT CONTROL is met when…
  • there is a contractual arrangement (e.g. a shareholders'/JV agreement); AND
  • decisions on the relevant activities need the unanimous consent of the parties sharing control — no single party can act alone (classic 50/50, or e.g. 3 parties at 33⅓% who must all agree, or a 50/40/10 where the two largest must agree).
Watch: if one party holds >50% and votes decide the relevant activities → that's control (IFRS 10), not joint control. A required quorum or simple-majority board is not joint control unless the same parties must combine and agree.

Step 2 — Classify it: JO or JV? (assess the parties' rights & obligations)

Work through the factors in this order. Each later factor can override the earlier one towards a joint operation. You can only conclude joint venture if the separate vehicle exists AND legal form, contract and other facts all point to rights to the net assets.

Decision flow:
① Separate vehicle?  NO → Joint OPERATION.  YES ↓
② Legal form gives parties rights to net assets (separation)?  NO → JO.  YES ↓
③ Contractual terms reverse it (give rights to assets / obligations for liabilities)?  YES → JO.  NO ↓
④ Other facts & circumstances (output to parties + parties fund the liabilities)?  YES → JO.  NO → Joint VENTURE.

Factor 1 — Is the arrangement structured through a SEPARATE VEHICLE?

  • A "separate vehicle" = a separately identifiable financial structure (a separate legal entity or an entity recognised by statute), e.g. a company, close corporation, partnership or trust.
  • No separate vehicle → always a JOINT OPERATION. Without a vehicle to "own" anything, the parties must hold the assets and owe the liabilities directly.
No-vehicle triggers (→ JO)"the parties jointly own and operate a pipeline / aircraft / oil field"; "each party uses its own assets and employees"; "no company was formed"; the arrangement is just a contract between the parties.
Example A — no vehicleX and Y jointly build and operate a gas pipeline under a contract; each owns a defined share of the pipe, takes its share of capacity, and pays its share of running costs. No separate vehicle → joint operation. Each recognises its share of the pipeline, the liabilities, and its costs.

Factor 2 — LEGAL FORM of the separate vehicle

  • Ask: does the legal form make the vehicle's assets & liabilities its own (separate from the parties), or do the parties keep direct rights/obligations?
  • Separation (the vehicle owns the assets & is liable for its own debts; creditors have no claim on the parties) → points to rights to net assets → JV.
  • No separation (the legal form gives parties direct rights to assets and makes them liable for the debts) → JO.
Legal-form triggers→ JV: "incorporated as a (Pty) Ltd", "limited liability", "the company owns the assets and is liable for its debts". → JO: "a partnership in which the partners are jointly & severally liable", "an unincorporated structure where the parties hold title to the assets".
Example B — legal form gives separation → JV (so far)P and Q each take 50% of NewCo (Pty) Ltd; NewCo owns the plant, contracts in its own name, and its creditors can only claim against NewCo. Legal form gives the parties rights to the net assets → points to a joint venture (now test the contract & other facts before concluding).
Example C — legal form gives NO separation → JOP and Q form a partnership in which each partner is directly liable for the partnership's debts and is entitled to a share of its specific assets. Legal form does not separate them → joint operation.

Factor 3 — CONTRACTUAL TERMS (can override the legal form towards JO)

  • Even where the legal form gives separation (a company → net-asset rights), the contract between the parties can hand them rights to the assets and obligations for the liabilities — which makes it a JO.
Contract triggers (→ JO, overriding the company form)
  • each party has a right to a specified share of the assets;
  • each party is obliged to fund / is liable for a share of the liabilities, or guarantees the vehicle's debts to third parties;
  • the parties are obliged to provide the cash to settle the vehicle's obligations;
  • the parties take all the output and the arrangement cannot sell to third parties.
If the contract is silent / consistent with net assets (parties share only profit, are not liable for debts) → still pointing to JV; go to Factor 4.
Example D — company, but contract reverses it → JOBack to NewCo (Pty) Ltd (Example B): the JV agreement states P and Q are each entitled to 50% of NewCo's assets and are each liable for 50% of its debts and guarantee its bank loan. Despite the company form, the contract gives rights to assets & obligations for liabilities → joint operation.

Factor 4 — OTHER FACTS & CIRCUMSTANCES (last override towards JO)

  • Even if legal form and contract point to net assets, the arrangement is a JO if it was designed so that, in substance, the parties have rights to the assets and must settle the liabilities. This is shown when BOTH:
  • (a) the arrangement's activities are primarily aimed at providing OUTPUT to the parties (the parties take substantially all the output); AND
  • (b) the arrangement depends on the parties to continuously settle its liabilities — its cash to pay creditors comes from the parties' purchases, not from third-party sales.
  • Both present → the parties effectively fund all the liabilities and consume all the assets → JO. If the vehicle sells to third parties and services its own debt, this override does not apply → JV.
Other-facts triggers (→ JO)"the output is sold only to the two parties", "priced at cost-plus so the vehicle just covers its costs", "the parties must buy the output / fund cash shortfalls", "the vehicle has no other customers and cannot pay its debts without the parties". (→ JV instead: "sells to external customers", "bears demand, inventory & credit risk", "raises and repays its own debt".)
Example E — company + neutral contract, but other facts → JOP and Q own JointCo (a company); the contract only shares profit. BUT JointCo manufactures a component sold exclusively to P and Q at cost-plus, has no other customers, and relies on their purchases to pay its suppliers. Activities provide output to the parties and it depends on them to settle liabilities → joint operation despite the company form & profit-sharing contract.
Example F — all factors point to net assets → JVP and Q own SellCo (Pty) Ltd; SellCo owns its assets, owes its own debts, sells to the open market, takes the demand/credit risk, and the parties are not liable for its debts and share only its profits. Separate vehicle + legal form + contract + other facts all give rights to the net assets → joint venture → equity method.
Golden rule for JO vs JVStart at "separate vehicle?" and keep asking "do the parties get the assets and owe the liabilities?" — if any factor says yes, it's a joint operation. Only when the vehicle and the legal form and the contract and the other facts all leave the parties with just the net assets/profit is it a joint venture.

6 · How to answer a discussion question (technique)

  1. State the standard & definition you are testing (start with IFRS 10 control).
  2. List each requirement as a sub-heading (Power · Variable returns · Link).
  3. Apply the facts to each — quote the trigger from the scenario ("X holds 45% while the rest is widely held and X appoints the board → de facto power").
  4. Conclude per requirement (met / not met) then overall (it is / isn't a subsidiary).
  5. If control fails, step down the tree: test joint control (IFRS 11), then significant influence (IAS 28).
  6. State the treatment that follows from your conclusion.
Mark-earning phrases: "control requires all three elements" · "the relevant activities are directed by…" · "this is a substantive right, not merely protective" · "applying the de facto control principle…" · "the parties require unanimous consent, so control is shared" · "rights to the net assets (JV) vs rights to the assets & liabilities (JO)" · "the ≥20% presumption is rebutted/supported because…".

7 · Worked discussion questions & model solutions

Q1 · Majority shares but no power
P holds 55% of Q's voting shares, but a shareholders' agreement gives R (a 30% holder) the right to direct Q's operating & financing policies and to appoint the majority of the board.

IFRS 10 — Power

  • Power = the current ability to direct the relevant activities. Here the contractual right sits with R, not P — substance over the 55% legal holding.
  • P has variable returns (dividends) but cannot direct the relevant activities → the power element fails.

Conclusion

P does not control Q. Test significant influence: P's 55% economic interest + likely board participation → associate (equity method). R, who has the power, would assess control.

LessonVoting % is only a starting point — contractual rights can shift power away from the majority holder.
Q2 · De facto control (<50%)
H owns 45% of S. The remaining 55% is held by thousands of shareholders, none owning more than 1%. H has elected the majority of the board at each of the last three AGMs and no other shareholder has organised to vote against it.

IFRS 10 — three elements

  • Power: although <50%, the other holdings are widely dispersed and H has practically directed the relevant activities (elects the board) → de facto control → power met.
  • Variable returns: dividends + change in value → met.
  • Link: H acts as principal and uses its power for its own returns → met.

Conclusion

All three met → S is a subsidiary → consolidate (recognise NCI of 55%).

Q3 · Potential voting rights
A owns 40% of B and holds options (currently exercisable, in-the-money) over a further 15% of B's shares. The other 45% is held by one competitor.

IFRS 10 — Power

  • Substantive potential voting rights (currently exercisable & in-the-money) are included when assessing power → A effectively commands 55% → power met.
  • If the options were not yet exercisable or deeply out-of-the-money → not substantive → exclude them → no control.

Conclusion

Treat the options as substantive → A controls B → subsidiary, consolidate.

Q4 · Significant influence (incl. rebutting <20%)
(a) C owns 25% of D, has 2 of 9 board seats and its finance director sits on D's dividend committee. (b) Alternatively, C owns only 18% but still has a board seat and helps set D's operating policy.

IAS 28 — significant influence

  • (a) 25% → ≥20% presumption applies, and it's confirmed by board representation + participation in dividend policy → significant influence met.
  • (b) 18% → the <20% presumption of "no influence" is rebutted by the board seat + policy participation → significant influence met.
  • In both, C does not control or jointly control → it only participates.

Conclusion

D is an associate → equity method.

Q5 · Joint arrangement — JV vs JO
E and F each own 50% of NewCo and their agreement requires unanimous consent for all relevant activities. (a) NewCo is a company that sells to third parties; the parties share its profits. (b) Alternatively, the contract says E and F each take 50% of NewCo's output and are each obliged to fund 50% of its costs.

IFRS 11 — joint control

  • Contractual arrangement + unanimous consent + 50/50 (no party acts alone) → joint control met.

Classify

  • (a) Separate vehicle, parties have rights to the net assets/profit → joint venture → equity method.
  • (b) The contract gives rights to the output (assets) and obligations for the costs (liabilities) → joint operation → each recognises its 50% share of assets, liabilities, income & expenses.
Q6 · Agent vs principal (no control)
G, a fund manager, holds 5% of a fund it manages, earns a market-related management fee, and the investors can remove G at any time by simple majority without cause.

IFRS 10 — the link (principal vs agent)

  • G makes decisions but the investors can remove it without cause and its economic exposure (5% + market fee) is small → G acts as an agent, not a principal.

Conclusion

G does not control the fund (no link between power & returns) → no consolidation; account for the 5% as an IFRS 9 investment.

MAF — Detailed Discussion-Question Guide

Financial Management + Management Accounting · each factor explained so you can write it out · SAICA IAC 2026 · T. Van Heerden
Method: ① state the factor → ② explain it (what it means) → ③ link to the scenario fact → ④ state the impact (profit/cash/risk/value/control) → ⑤ give both sides → ⑥ recommend & conclude. ~1 mark per applied point; support words with a number where you can.
PART A · FINANCIAL MANAGEMENT

A1 · Risk — framework & types

The framework

  • Risk process — Identify what can go wrong → assess on likelihood × impact (heat map) → respond → monitor & report to the risk/audit committee. Distinguish inherent risk (before controls) from residual risk (after controls/hedges).
  • Risk appetite vs capacity — appetite = how much risk the board is willing to take; capacity = how much it can afford to absorb. The chosen response must sit within appetite.
  • The 4 T responses — Terminate (stop the activity, when risk is too high & not core); Treat (controls/hedges to reduce it to an acceptable level); Transfer (insure, hedge, or outsource to a party better able to bear it); Tolerate (accept it, when within appetite or the cost of action exceeds the benefit).

Risk types to identify (what each means + the scenario clue)

  • Foreign-exchange (currency) risk — the risk that exchange-rate moves change the rand value of foreign transactions/assets. Clue: imports, exports, foreign loans or a foreign subsidiary. Impact: a weaker rand raises import costs / a stronger rand cuts export & translated profits.
  • Interest-rate risk — the risk that rate changes raise finance costs or reduce returns. Clue: floating/variable-rate debt. Impact: rising rates increase interest expense and reduce profit & cash.
  • Commodity/input-price risk — exposure to volatile input prices. Clue: reliance on fuel, steel, maize. Impact: price spikes squeeze margins if they can't be passed on.
  • Equity/investment-price risk — fall in the value of investments held. Clue: share portfolio / listed investments.
  • Credit risk — customers/counterparties default. Clue: large or concentrated debtors, weak economy. Impact: bad debts & cash-flow strain.
  • Liquidity risk — inability to pay debts as they fall due. Clue: short-term debt > current assets, low current/quick ratio, high overdraft.
  • Operational risk — losses from failed people, processes, systems or fraud. Clue: manual systems, poor controls, key-person reliance.
  • Strategic/business risk — revenue volatility from competition, regulation, demand or a key-customer reliance. Going-concern risk — losses, covenant breach or negative equity threatening survival.
Application styleForex risk → the company has just acquired a foreign subsidiary and may suffer unfavourable exchange-rate fluctuations that reduce its profitability; it should treat/transfer the exposure using forward contracts or a natural hedge.

A2 · Foreign-exchange risk & hedging

Types of exposure

  • Transaction exposure — a committed future cash flow in foreign currency (a payable or receivable); a rate move creates a real cash gain/loss on settlement. Most commonly hedged.
  • Translation exposure — arises on consolidating a foreign subsidiary (net assets & results retranslated); it is an accounting effect (FCTR in equity), not a cash flow — often left unhedged.
  • Economic exposure — the long-run effect of exchange-rate changes on the company's competitiveness & value; hardest to hedge (manage via diversifying markets/sourcing).

Hedging instruments — what each is & when to use it

  • Forward exchange contract (FEC) — a binding contract to buy/sell currency at a fixed rate on a set future date. Pros: complete certainty, no premium. Cons: it's an obligation, so you lose any favourable move, and you must settle even if the underlying deal falls away; counterparty risk. Use: a known amount & date.
  • Currency option — the right, not the obligation, to exchange at a strike rate. Pros: protects the downside but lets you walk away and keep the upside. Cons: you pay a premium upfront. Use: when the cash flow is uncertain or you want to keep upside.
  • Money-market hedge — replicate a forward using borrowing/deposits (e.g. for a payable: borrow rand now, convert, and deposit the foreign currency so it grows to the amount due). Use: when forwards are unavailable/expensive; fixes the cost today.
  • Futures — standardised, exchange-traded contracts with margin calls. Pros: liquid, tradable. Cons: standardised sizes/dates rarely match exactly (basis risk); margin cash flows.
  • Natural hedge / matching — match foreign-currency inflows with outflows (e.g. fund a foreign subsidiary with foreign debt, or invoice purchases & sales in the same currency). Cheapest — no instrument needed.
  • Netting — offset intra-group payables and receivables so only the net amount is exposed.
  • Leading & lagging — pay early (lead) if the foreign currency is expected to strengthen; delay (lag) if it's expected to weaken.
Forecasting ratesInterest-rate parity: the forward rate differs from spot by the interest-rate differential between the two currencies. Purchasing-power parity: over time currencies adjust for inflation differences (higher-inflation currency weakens).

A3 · Interest-rate risk & hedging

  • Interest-rate swap — exchange a floating interest stream for a fixed one (or vice versa) with a counterparty. Use: on floating-rate debt when rates are expected to rise or you want certainty of finance cost.
  • Forward rate agreement (FRA) — fixes the interest rate on a notional future loan/deposit for a set period; cash-settled for the difference between the agreed and actual rate.
  • Interest-rate cap — an option setting a maximum rate (pay a premium) → protects against rises while keeping the benefit of falls. Collar — buy a cap and sell a floor to reduce the premium, giving up the benefit below the floor.
  • Fixed/floating mix — match the proportion of fixed debt to how predictable cash flows are and the rate outlook; diversifies interest risk.

A4 · Hedging policy & treasury

  • Should we hedge at all? — Weigh the cost of hedging against the size & volatility of the exposure and materiality. Arguments for: reduces cash-flow volatility, lowers distress risk, aids planning/budgeting, protects covenants. Arguments against: shareholders can diversify themselves, hedging has a cost and may not add value, and it removes upside. Never use derivatives to speculate.
  • Dangers of derivatives — leverage magnifies losses; counterparty/credit risk; complexity & valuation difficulty; mark-to-market causes earnings volatility → need a board-approved policy, position limits, segregation of duties & authorisation.
  • Treasury: centralised vs decentralised — central treasury pools cash, nets exposures, secures better rates & expertise and improves control; decentralised is more responsive to local conditions and motivates local managers.
  • Country/political risk — expropriation, exchange controls/blocked funds, instability or regulatory change. Mitigate: political-risk insurance, borrow locally (so loss falls on local lenders), JV with a local partner, phase the investment.

A5 · Debt vs Equity (& conversions)

Why choose DEBT

  • Cheaper — interest is tax-deductible (a tax shield) and lenders bear less risk so demand a lower return → reduces WACC. (Only valuable if the company is profitable enough to use the tax shield.)
  • No dilution — existing shareholders keep control and EPS isn't diluted.
  • Discipline — fixed repayments reduce wasteful spending (free-cash-flow agency cost).
  • Best when — cash flows are stable & predictable, gearing is currently low, and security is available.

Why choose EQUITY

  • No fixed obligation — dividends are discretionary → lower financial risk & less liquidity strain.
  • Protects capacity — doesn't raise gearing, so it preserves covenants, credit rating and future borrowing ability.
  • Best when — cash flows are volatile, the company is already highly geared, the project is risky/long-term, or there's no security.
  • Drawbacks — dilutes control & EPS, higher required return, issue costs, and may signal to the market that managers think the shares are over-valued.

Always assess & the conversions

  • Test each option for: gearing & covenant headroom, EPS impact, control/ownership, WACC effect, and ability to service the cash flows.
  • Convertible instruments — debt that can convert into shares: carries a lower coupon (the investor pays for the conversion option), defers & potentially self-liquidates the equity (no immediate dilution); risk = if the share price doesn't reach the conversion price you must redeem in cash.
  • Debt-to-equity swap (distress) — creditors exchange their debt for shares: cuts the interest burden and gearing, improves solvency & liquidity and avoids default; but it dilutes existing shareholders and may hand creditors voting control.
  • Matching principle — fund long-term assets & permanent working capital with long-term finance, and fluctuating working capital with short-term finance; a mismatch creates refinancing/liquidity risk.
ApplicationA highly geared company in distress is offered a debt-to-equity swap → it improves solvency and removes interest, but it dilutes existing owners and may give creditors control → weigh survival against loss of control.

A6 · Sources of finance & special transactions

  • Choosing a source — consider: cost (interest/return + issue costs); risk/gearing effect; control/dilution; security required; term (match to the asset's life); flexibility (early repayment, drawdown); availability (size, market access); and the effect on cash flow & covenants.
  • Short-term: overdraft (flexible, repayable on demand), trade credit (free but loses discounts), factoring/invoice discounting (cash now for a fee). Long-term: bank loans, bonds/debentures, leases, equity, retained earnings.
  • Rights issue — new shares offered to existing holders pro-rata at a discount; pre-emptive so no dilution if everyone takes up; cheaper than a public issue. TERP (theoretical ex-rights price) = (value of existing shares + rights proceeds) ÷ total shares after; the value of a right = TERP − issue price.
  • Share buyback — the company repurchases its own shares: returns surplus cash, increases EPS & can support the share price, and signals confidence/undervaluation; but it reduces equity → raises gearing.
  • Scrip/capitalisation dividend — shares instead of cash: conserves cash and can be tax-efficient; causes slight dilution.
  • IPO / listing — Pros: access to large capital, share liquidity, profile & a "currency" for acquisitions. Cons: cost, heavy regulation & disclosure, loss of control, short-term market pressure, takeover vulnerability.
  • B-BBEE deal — improves the BEE scorecard (ownership/procurement points); usually funded by vendor financing or notional vesting; dilutes existing owners; has lock-in/transfer restrictions; must be commercially sustainable.
  • Private equity / venture capital — growth or buy-out capital plus expertise; the investor wants an exit (typically 3–7 years), board seats & control rights; LBO structures use heavy debt (higher risk & return).
  • MBO / MBI / LBO — management buys the business, often debt-funded: aligns incentives but the high gearing is risky.
  • SME / start-up finance — limited security & track record → owner equity, retained earnings, bank loans (with personal guarantees), grants/incentives, angel/VC, crowdfunding, trade credit.

A7 · Cost of capital / WACC

  • WACC — the weighted-average (using market values) after-tax cost of all funding; it is the correct discount rate only for projects of average risk financed in the existing structure.
  • Cost of equity (CAPM) — Re = Rf + β(Rm − Rf); β measures systematic (undiversifiable) risk; a higher β means a higher required return.
  • Cost of debt — use the after-tax rate, Kd × (1 − tax), at the current market yield to maturity (not the historic coupon).
  • Risk-adjusted / divisional rate — a project or division with different risk needs its own rate: take a comparable listed "pure-play" company's equity β, ungear it (strip out its financial risk to get an asset β), then regear it at the project's own gearing → a project β → CAPM → project discount rate.
  • Critiques — when NOT to use the group WACC: the project's risk differs from the company average; the capital structure is changing; book (not market) weights were used; β/market data is unreliable or the company is unlisted; foreign or otherwise different-risk projects; significant financing side-effects → use APV (value the project as if all-equity, then add the PV of financing effects such as debt tax shields and issue costs).

A8 · Investment appraisal

  • NPV — PV of incremental after-tax cash flows discounted at the cost of capital; positive = adds value. Best method (absolute money value, uses time value, considers all cash flows).
  • IRR — the rate where NPV = 0; accept if > cost of capital. Weaknesses: multiple IRRs with unconventional cash flows, an unrealistic reinvestment assumption, and it can rank mutually exclusive projects incorrectly versus NPV.
  • Payback / discounted payback — time to recover the outlay; a simple liquidity/risk screen but ignores cash flows after payback (and, unless discounted, the time value).
  • Relevant cash flows — include only incremental, future, after-tax flows: add working-capital investment (and its release at the end) and opportunity costs; exclude sunk costs, allocated/committed overheads, depreciation (non-cash — but include its tax effect), and financing flows (already in the discount rate).
  • Inflation — be consistent: discount real (today's-price) cash flows at the real rate, or money/nominal cash flows at the money rate (Fisher: 1+money = (1+real)(1+inflation)).
  • Tax — tax the operating profits (allow for a payment lag), claim capital allowances/wear-and-tear (a tax shield), and tax recoupments/scrapping on disposal.
  • Capital rationing — limited funds: if single-period & projects are divisible, rank by the profitability index (NPV ÷ initial outlay); if indivisible, test feasible combinations for the highest total NPV; multi-period → linear programming.
  • Lease vs buy — a financing decision: compare the PV of after-tax leasing cash flows with the PV of buying (borrowing), discounted at the after-tax cost of debt; remember lost capital allowances if you lease.
  • Replacement — when to replace an asset: compute the equivalent annual cost of each replacement cycle and pick the lowest.
  • Foreign project — either discount foreign cash flows at a foreign risk-adjusted rate then convert at spot, or convert each year's flows at expected rates then discount at the home rate; add country risk, withholding tax and blocked-funds issues.
  • Real options & qualitative — NPV ignores managerial flexibility: options to expand (follow-on), delay/defer, or abandon add value. Also weigh strategic fit, ESG/6-capitals, employees, reputation, customer/supplier relationships, B-BBEE, and whether it can be financed within covenants.
ApplicationThe NPV is positive but small → sensitivity analysis shows it turns negative if sales volume drops only ~8%, and the forecast assumes optimistic growth → recommend cautiously, subject to firmer demand evidence.

A9 · Valuation (business / shares)

  • DCF — discount free cash flows: FCFF at WACC → enterprise value; FCFE at Ke → equity value. Theoretically best, but very sensitive to the growth rate, discount rate and terminal value → present a range.
  • Relative multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, P/B) — quick & market-based, but need genuinely comparable companies (size, risk, growth, sector) and adjustment for accounting-policy & capital-structure differences; P/E is unreliable when earnings are volatile or negative.
  • Net asset value — assets less liabilities; a floor/minimum value; ignores internally generated goodwill and future earnings; use fair values not book values; suits asset-heavy businesses or a liquidation.
  • Dividend growth model — P0 = D1 ÷ (Ke − g); for minority, dividend-paying stakes; extremely sensitive to g (and needs Ke > g).
  • Adjustments — add a control premium for a controlling stake; apply a minority and/or marketability/liquidity discount (unlisted); add surplus/non-operating assets; add synergies for a specific buyer; deduct contingent liabilities.

A10 · Capital structure & dividend policy

Capital structure / gearing

  • Gearing (debt ÷ equity, or debt ÷ (debt+equity)) — high gearing magnifies returns to equity but raises financial risk & volatility, distress/bankruptcy risk, covenant pressure, and reduces flexibility.
  • Interest cover (EBIT ÷ interest) — measures the ability to service debt; low cover signals vulnerability if profits fall.
  • Theory hooks — trade-off: optimal gearing balances the debt tax shield against rising distress & agency costs; pecking order: firms prefer internal funds → then debt → then equity (information asymmetry); signalling: issuing equity may signal over-valuation. Moderate debt lowers WACC; too much raises it as risk grows.

Dividend policy — factors

  • Cash & liquidity — only pay what's affordable without harming operations or breaching the s4/s46 solvency & liquidity test.
  • Investment opportunities — retain profits to fund positive-NPV projects (residual theory); pay out if there are none.
  • Signalling & clientele — investors read dividends as signals (a cut implies trouble); a stable, predictable policy is valued; different shareholder "clienteles" prefer income vs growth.
  • Other — tax (20% dividends tax & the shareholder mix), loan covenants/legal limits, and control (paying dividends then raising new equity dilutes owners).

A11 · Working-capital management

  • Cash conversion cycle = inventory days + debtor days − creditor days; it's how long cash is tied up in operations — shorten it to free up cash.
  • Overtrading (under-capitalisation) — growing sales faster than the working capital to support it → can't pay suppliers despite being profitable → liquidity crisis. Signs: rising sales, falling liquidity ratios, a growing overdraft & lengthening creditor days.
  • Over-capitalisation — too much tied up in idle cash/stock/debtors → a poor return on assets.
  • Financing policy — aggressive (fund even permanent WC with short-term money — cheap but refinancing/liquidity risk); conservative (use long-term funds for fluctuating WC — safe but costly); matching (match maturities of funding to assets).
  • Debtors — tighten credit vetting/terms; offer settlement discounts (compare the cost of the discount to the benefit of faster cash & fewer bad debts); use an age analysis & active collection; factoring gives cash now for a fee and some loss of control.
  • Inventory — EOQ = √(2 × order cost × annual demand ÷ holding cost per unit) minimises total ordering + holding cost; evaluate bulk discounts (saving vs extra holding cost); JIT cuts holding cost but raises stock-out & supplier-reliability risk.
  • Cash — the Baumol model (orders cash like EOQ) and Miller-Orr model (upper/lower limits with a return point) set optimal balances; invest surpluses short-term and keep a buffer/overdraft for uncertainty.
  • Creditors — take the full free-credit period, but weigh the loss of early-settlement discounts and supplier goodwill if you stretch them.

A12 · M&A, restructuring & distress

  • Reasons for M&A — synergy (cost: economies of scale & removing duplication; revenue: cross-selling & market power), faster growth than organic, market share/entry, diversification (though shareholders can diversify themselves), access to technology/skills/brands/distribution, and tax benefits.
  • Types — horizontal (same industry: scale & market power, but competition-authority risk), vertical (a supplier or customer: secure supply/distribution), conglomerate (unrelated: diversification, but risk of losing focus).
  • Form of consideration — cash (certain value to the seller, but raises the acquirer's gearing) vs shares (no cash outflow, but dilutes the acquirer and makes the seller share future risk; watch the EPS effect / "bootstrapping"); earn-outs bridge price gaps & retain sellers.
  • Why deals fail / risks — overpaying (winner's curse), over-estimated synergies, culture clash, poor integration, management distraction, the debt burden, and competition/regulatory rejection.
  • Price range — minimum = the target's standalone value; maximum = standalone + synergies; negotiate to share the synergy — don't pay it all away.
  • Takeover defences — poison pill, white knight, crown-jewels defence, golden parachutes, regulatory appeal.
  • Demerger / unbundling / divestiture — splitting off a division can unlock value, sharpen focus & accountability, remove the conglomerate discount and suit different investors (links to s46 corporate-rules tax relief).
  • Turnaround / business rescue — assess viability first; then restructure (reschedule debt or a debt-equity swap), dispose of non-core assets, cut costs, secure new funding and possibly replace management; consider the going-concern indicators and whether rescue is reasonably likely to succeed.

A13 · Markets, governance & integrated thinking

  • Market efficiency (EMH) — weak form: prices reflect past prices; semi-strong: all public information (broadly the JSE); strong: all information incl. private. Implication: if at least semi-strong efficient, cosmetic accounting or timing won't fool the market, new issues are fairly priced, and you can't consistently "beat" the market on public information.
  • Agency theory & governance — managers (agents) may not act in owners' (principals') interests → mitigate with value-aligned incentives, monitoring, King IV governance, an effective board/audit committee, and treasury authorisation limits.
  • Shareholder vs stakeholder value & the 6 capitals — financial, manufactured, intellectual, human, social & relationship, and natural capital. Integrated thinking weighs the trade-offs between them and the value created or eroded over time; consider ESG/reputational risk, King IV "apply & explain", and the triple context (economy, society, environment).
PART B · MANAGEMENT ACCOUNTING

B1 · Costing systems — which to use & why

  • Absorption vs marginal costing — absorption includes fixed production overhead in the product cost (and therefore in closing inventory), so profit changes with inventory movements; it's required for IFRS/external reporting. Marginal charges only variable cost to the product and treats fixed cost as a period cost — better for short-term decisions and it avoids inventory-driven profit distortion.
  • Activity-based costing (ABC) — assigns overheads using cost drivers (activities) instead of a single volume base. Use when overheads are large and products are diverse/low-volume → more accurate product costs & pricing and better cost control. Cons: costly & complex to set up and maintain; driver choice is subjective.
  • Throughput accounting / theory of constraints — treats only material as variable; maximise throughput (sales − material cost) through the bottleneck; rank products by throughput per bottleneck-hour and elevate the constraint.
  • Target costing — start from the market-determined selling price, subtract the required profit margin to get the target cost; if the current cost is higher, close the gap through design/value engineering. Used for new, competitive products.
  • Life-cycle costing — accumulates all costs over the product's whole life (R&D, launch, growth, decline, disposal) → better pricing & a true view of profitability, recognising large upfront/end-of-life costs.
  • Kaizen costing — continuous, incremental cost reduction during production. Quality costs — prevention + appraisal (conformance) vs internal + external failure (non-conformance): spending on prevention reduces costly failures. Environmental MA — identify & manage environmental costs.

B2 · Decision-making (relevant costing)

  • Relevant cost = future, incremental, cash — include opportunity cost (the contribution forgone from the next-best use of a resource); exclude sunk costs, committed/allocated fixed costs, and depreciation/notional charges.
  • Make vs buy / outsource — compare the incremental cost to make (variable + avoidable fixed) with the external buy-in price; then weigh qualitative factors: quality & reliability, supplier dependence, spare capacity, control of IP/quality, staff morale, strategic importance and confidentiality.
  • Special / one-off order — accept if the price exceeds the incremental cost AND there's spare capacity AND it won't cannibalise normal sales or undercut existing customers' prices; if capacity is full, include the opportunity cost of displaced work.
  • Shutdown / discontinue — compare the contribution lost from closing with the fixed/specific costs saved; also consider knock-on effects on other products, staff & reputation, redundancy costs, and whether the decision is reversible.
  • Limiting / key factor — when a resource is scarce, maximise contribution per unit of the limiting factor (or throughput per bottleneck-hour) and rank production accordingly; then overlay qualitative factors (e.g. keeping key-customer products).
  • Joint products / further processing — the joint (pre-split-off) costs are irrelevant to the further-processing decision; process further only if the incremental revenue exceeds the incremental further-processing cost.

B3 · Decisions under risk & uncertainty

  • Sensitivity analysis — change one variable to find how far it can move before the decision reverses; pinpoints the critical variable and where to focus management. It doesn't use probabilities.
  • Scenario analysis — change several variables together into best/base/worst cases → shows the range of outcomes.
  • Expected values — Σ(probability × outcome): a probability-weighted average. Good for repeated decisions; ignores the decision-maker's risk attitude and the impact of a one-off bad outcome, and probabilities are only estimates.
  • Decision trees — map sequential decisions & chance events and "roll back" to choose; useful for staged/phased investments.
  • Simulation (Monte Carlo) — models many variables with probability distributions → a full distribution of possible outcomes.
  • Value of (perfect/imperfect) information — the maximum worth paying for information equals the increase in expected value from making better decisions with it.
  • Risk-attitude rules — maximin (choose the best worst-case — cautious), maximax (best best-case — optimistic), minimax regret (minimise the largest regret).

B4 · CVP & pricing

CVP

  • Breakeven units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit; breakeven revenue = fixed ÷ C/S ratio; target-profit volume = (fixed + target profit) ÷ contribution per unit; margin of safety = how far sales can fall before a loss. Multi-product uses a weighted-average contribution at a fixed sales mix.
  • Limitations — assumes linear costs & revenue, a constant sales mix, and that everything produced is sold → it's a short-run approximation.

Pricing strategies — what each means & when to use it

  • Cost-plus pricing — add a mark-up to cost. Pros: simple, ensures costs are covered, useful when costs are known & demand is hard to gauge. Cons: ignores what customers will pay and competitors, and it's circular (price affects volume affects unit cost).
  • Market / value-based pricing — set the price on the value to the customer and what the market will bear. Pros: maximises profit. Cons: needs good market & customer knowledge.
  • Penetration pricing — deliberately set a low launch price to enter a market, win share/volume quickly, deter competitors and exploit economies of scale. Use for price-sensitive mass markets & new entrants; you sacrifice short-term margin and it can start a price war.
  • Price skimming — set a high initial price aimed at customers willing to pay (early adopters), recover R&D quickly, then lower it over time as competition arrives. Use for innovative/unique products with little competition and price-inelastic early demand.
  • Price discrimination — charge different prices to different customers/segments/times for essentially the same product (e.g. student vs full price, peak vs off-peak, geographic). It captures more consumer surplus. Requires: separable markets, the ability to prevent resale between them, and different price elasticities.
  • Other tactics — premium (prestige) pricing, economy/no-frills, psychological (R99,99), loss-leader (cheap item drives other sales), product-line/bundle pricing, and complementary "razor-and-blades" pricing.
  • Competitor & strategic positioning — consider competitors' likely reaction and current prices, your brand position (premium vs value), the product's life-cycle stage, demand elasticity, regulation, and your overall strategy (low-cost leadership vs differentiation).

B5 · Budgeting & variance analysis

  • Investigating variances — consider the size & %, controllability (is it the manager's responsibility?), the trend over time, interdependence (e.g. buying cheaper material causes an adverse usage/quality variance), and whether the original standard was realistic; only investigate where the benefit exceeds the cost.
  • Standard variance types — price/rate & usage/efficiency (materials, labour); variable & fixed overhead (expenditure, efficiency, volume); sales price & volume.
  • Advanced variances — materials/labour mix & yield (where inputs are substitutable); sales mix & quantity; and planning vs operational variances (separate the part caused by a flawed original standard, which is uncontrollable "planning", from the part management could control, "operational" — giving a fairer evaluation).
  • Budget behaviour & approaches — participative (bottom-up) budgets improve motivation but risk budgetary slack; imposed (top-down) are faster but can demotivate; always compare actuals to a flexed budget (flexed to the actual activity level) for fairness; rolling budgets stay current; beyond budgeting and zero-based (justify from zero) vs incremental.

B6 · Performance measurement

  • Ratio analysis — profitability (GP%, OP%, ROE, ROCE), liquidity (current, quick), gearing & interest cover, efficiency (inventory/debtor/creditor days, asset turnover), and investor ratios (EPS, P/E, dividend yield); always compare to prior year, budget & industry. Limitations: different accounting policies, one-off items, inflation, year-end window-dressing, and the fact that ratios ignore non-financial & qualitative factors and are interrelated.
  • ROI vs RI vs EVA — ROI = profit ÷ capital employed: easy & comparable, but can be dysfunctional (a manager rejects a project that beats the WACC but is below their current divisional ROI). Residual income = profit − (capital × cost of capital): promotes goal congruence (accept any positive-RI project) but is an absolute figure (size bias). EVA = adjusted NOPAT − (capital × WACC): links directly to shareholder value and corrects accounting distortions.
  • Balanced scorecard — four perspectives: financial, customer, internal process, and learning & growth → balances short vs long term and financial vs non-financial; it needs good measures and clear cause-and-effect links.
  • Divisional performance & transfer pricing — pick a basis that gives goal congruence, fair evaluation and divisional autonomy. Market-based is best where a competitive external market exists; cost-based (full or variable cost ± mark-up) risks passing on inefficiency; negotiated depends on bargaining power. General rule: minimum transfer price = the selling division's variable/marginal cost + its opportunity cost; maximum = the lowest external purchase price. Watch divisional motivation and cross-border tax (s31).
  • Other — benchmarking against best practice, well-chosen KPIs, the controllability principle (assess managers only on what they control), and non-financial/ESG measures.
